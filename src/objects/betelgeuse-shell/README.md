# Betelgeuse circumstellar volumes

## Sources

This package draws the material around Betelgeuse as four prepared volumes, one lens each, around the star's own sphere:

- `zimpol-v`: the polarised dust VLT/SPHERE-ZIMPOL measured on 3 December 2024.
- `emission-2020`: the 4 micrometre light outside the photosphere in the VLTI/MATISSE reconstruction of February 2020, the same image the sphere is painted from.
- `sio-2023`: silicon monoxide around the star in an ALMA line cube of August 2023, the one lens placed in depth by its own measured velocities.
- `veil-2019-12`: the dust clump Montargès et al. (2021) fitted to the Great Dimming of December 2019, drawn from their published numbers.

Each has its own section below.

## The 2024 polarised dust

The shell is the degree of linear polarisation around Betelgeuse measured by VLT/SPHERE-ZIMPOL in the V band on 3 December 2024, from the ESO Phase 3 collection `BETELGEUSE-B` (programme 114.28H9.001, released 2026-08-19) that accompanies Montargès et al. (2026, [A&A 711, L12](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202661023)). Two of the four released products are used: the pipeline intensity image `SPHERE_ZIMPOL_Betelgeuse_P1_V_phase3.fits` (ESO `ADP.2026-08-19T13:19:07.655`) and its ancillary degree-of-linear-polarisation map `SPHERE_ZIMPOL_Betelgeuse_P1_V_DOLP.fits` (`ADP.2026-08-19T13:19:07.656`), both 1024 × 1024 pixels of 3.6 milliarcseconds, north up and east left by their WCS. The N_I pair is kept beside them and not shown: its polarisation is a tight ring at the limb, the pattern a resolved disc's own edge produces, while the V map shows the patches from one to 4.5 stellar radii that Kervella et al. (2016) and the release paper attribute to dust.

**Placement.** The cube's centre is Betelgeuse's own scene origin, the point its sphere is drawn at: the prepared bank's frame origin and the star's world frame agree to 794 metres, one and a half billionths of a stellar radius, which is float round-trip, and one volume unit is exactly one stellar radius. So the sphere is at the centre of the cube by construction, and the only question that can be got wrong is where the star sits inside the map.

**The two released products are not pixel-aligned to each other,** although they carry one WCS, and getting that wrong moved the whole envelope. The intensity image puts the star at pixel (513.00, 429.09): its peak, its half-maximum silhouette centroid and its ten-percent centroid all agree there. The degree map marks the stellar disc instead by setting 93 pixels to exact zero, a disc of 0.93 stellar radii centred at (502.40, 428.43), and nothing else in that frame is exactly zero. The two centres are 10.6 pixels apart, 38 milliarcseconds, 1.8 stellar radii. A first version measured the star on the intensity image and sampled the degree map about that point, which put the polarised envelope nearly two stellar radii off the star. Each product is now read about its own centre.

**Checked against the published figure.** Fig. B.1 of Montargès et al. 2026 is shipped in `source/previews/` and can be registered against the released degree map directly. Its five ticks an axis are 159.5 image pixels apart and label 50-milliarcsecond steps, which calibrates the panel at 0.31348 mas per pixel with no assumption: the panel spans 240.4 mas and the red circle marking the star is 21.0 mas in radius, against this package's stellar radius of 21.15 mas. Fitting the panel to the degree map at that fixed scale peaks at a normalised cross-correlation of 0.798 with the figure's origin at degree-map pixel (501.53, 430.31), and the peak is single and symmetric, falling to 0.09 within eight pixels either way. The mirrored fits reach only 0.455 and 0.421, which settles the grid's own handedness: increasing image column is west, as the sampling assumes. Against that origin the first version's sampling centre scored 0.282 and the present one scores 0.771, 2.1 pixels or 0.35 stellar radii from the paper's own marker, which is the precision of registering a smoothed figure.

**What is genuinely lopsided is the light, not the star.** The centroid of the drawn brightness between one and 4.5 radii lies about a stellar radius from the star, because the envelope is clumpy; one quadrant of the one-to-three-radius ring carries roughly a third of the polarised light of the other three. The published figure shows the same asymmetry.

**Depth: a shape fitted to the profile, not the image pushed backwards.** The map is a sky-plane image with no third axis, and an extrusion along the line of sight is not a shape. This package instead asks which simple envelope, placed around the star, projects to the radial profile that was measured. The azimuthal average between one and six stellar radii is fitted against a spherical shell of free radius and gaussian thickness, against the steady outflow a constant mass-loss rate gives, and against constant depth, which is what an extrusion assumes. The shell wins and is what is drawn:

| envelope | residual against a profile of 1.41e-2 |
|---|---|
| spherical shell, radius 3.50 R★, gaussian thickness 1.20 R★ | 4.28e-3 |
| steady outflow, ρ ∝ r^−0.70 | 6.23e-3 |
| constant depth, what an extrusion assumes | 6.94e-3 |

Each sky column is then spread along that envelope and normalised so it reproduces its own measured degree, which puts a patch at the shell's radius rather than smeared through the box. The envelope is symmetric in depth, so every patch is drawn both in front of the star and behind it; one image cannot say which, and nothing here pretends to. The author refuses to write the grid if the shell is not the best of the three. The map is floor-subtracted (the median degree between 8 and 12 radii, 0.55 percent, is instrumental) and carried on the same zero-to-0.10 scale as the published figure's colourbar; the disc within one radius and everything fainter than three thousandths of the stellar peak are removed; the map tapers out between 4.5 and six radii. The shell radius is inferred, not measured.

## The 4 micrometre light outside the disc

The VLTI/MATISSE reconstruction that paints Betelgeuse's sphere (see [Betelgeuse](../betelgeuse/README.md)) carries a fifth of its flux outside the published 42.45 mas disc. On the sphere that light would be a flat plate behind the body, so it is given a shape here the same way the polarisation is. It is resolved emission, not the instrument: the light falls by a factor of 2.3 across the first 4 mas beam beyond the limb, 3.0 across the second and 3.2 across the next two, where a gaussian of that beam would fall by orders of magnitude, and it flattens into a shoulder between 42 and 50 mas, which a beam skirt cannot produce.

The disc and the first beam beyond it are excluded: inside the disc the sphere is drawn, and within one beam of it the light is the star's own edge smeared by the beam. Over the 1.19 to 2.36 stellar radii that remain, the envelope that best projects to the measured profile is a steep steady outflow, density as r^−7.95, leaving 2.99e-6 against 1.50e-5 for the constant depth an extrusion assumes, on a profile whose own rms is 2.09e-5. Like the 2024 shell it is a shape fitted to one projection and symmetric in depth. Its colour is the reconstruction's own heat scale, the one the sphere already carries, because it is the same quantity.

## The silicon monoxide around the star

**The data.** ALMA project 2022.A.00026.S observed Betelgeuse in band 6 from 3 to 27 August 2023 ([archive](https://almascience.org/aq/?result_view=observation&projectCode=2022.A.00026.S)). Its spectral window 27 carries SiO v=0 J=5–4 at 217.105 GHz, the molecule silicate dust condenses from. The pipeline cube of that window is 72 GB. The archive's SODA service cuts it on the server, and the request recorded as `ALMA_SIO.cutout` in the authoring script returns 522 MB about the observation's phase centre. `tools/objects/source-authoring/betelgeuse-shell/reduce-alma-sio.mts` keeps a box 300 mas either way of the phase centre and the 47 channels of 1.35 km/s that hold the line. The pipeline subtracts the continuum before it images a cube, so the reducer removes only the residual: the median of the 1738 channels more than 120 km/s from the line. It also cuts the same box out of the member's continuum image, made with the same calibration. Both land in `.local/betelgeuse-shell/observations/` beside the package's other downloads, which stay out of git as every volume's do: the manifest pins them by hash, the source cache mirrors the ones no publisher serves byte for byte, and the reducer fails unless it reproduces the pinned bytes. The beam is 30 × 22 mas, 1.4 by 1.0 stellar radii.

**Where the star is.** The archive references both images to the phase centre, and the phase centre is not the star. The line cube cannot show the star, because its continuum has been removed; the continuum image shows it at 121 times its noise. The centroid of its half-maximum lies 84.9 mas west and 57.2 mas south of the phase centre, 102 mas or 4.8 stellar radii, and every SiO position is measured from it. The line checks this independently: absorption needs the star's continuum behind the gas, and the centroid of the SiO absorption falls 2.2 mas from the continuum star. A first version took the phase centre for the star, which turned the bright side of the ring below into a lone clump 3.3 radii to the south-west; the [ledger](investigations.json) records it.

**What it shows.** In front of the star the line absorbs from −21.3 to +16.4 km/s about the star's velocity, deepest 23 mJy per beam: gas between us and the star, leaving it on the blue side and falling back on the red. Inside the star's continuum footprint, emission cannot be told from that absorption and is not drawn. Outside it, moment masking (Dame 2011: the cube smoothed by its beam and three channels, cut at five times its noise and grown by the smoothing) keeps 331 pixels within six stellar radii, 22 beams carrying 0.55 Jy km/s. They form a lopsided ring, brightest 2.4 stellar radii from the star, with its weight 1.2 radii toward position angle 122 degrees, east-south-east. The star's velocity, the flux-weighted mean of the ring, is 5.1 km/s LSRK.

**Depth.** The blue edge of the absorption is the fastest gas in front of the star coming toward us, which is how a P Cygni profile gives a wind's terminal speed: 21.3 km/s, 22.0 with half a channel. Every channel of every pixel is placed along the line of sight on a spherical outflow at that speed, where a parcel at sky distance s moving at v lies at depth s v / √(u² − v²). Reading each channel on its own keeps the near and the far side of a line of sight apart; one mean velocity per pixel would average them onto the sky plane. 99.6 percent of the ring's flux lands inside the grid, the deepest 5.2 stellar radii along the line of sight, and 0.01 percent is faster than the flow and cannot be placed on it. Each sample is drawn as a gaussian a quarter of the beam's minor axis in standard deviation. The colour is viridis on the fraction of the grid's peak, marked false colour.

**What is assumed, and what is left out.** The sky positions and velocities are measured. That every parcel flows straight out at one speed is not: the absorption's red side is gas falling back, which this model places as if it were leaving, and the inner wind of a red supergiant is turbulent, so the depths are the model's. A further 29 percent of the masked flux lies 8 to 14 stellar radii to the north, beside a continuum peak five times the noise, beyond the six radii the volume holds. It is not drawn; whether it is circumstellar gas or an artefact of the bright star in images made without self-calibration is open in the ledger.

## The December 2019 clump

This lens is not an image. It is the dust clump Montargès et al. (2021, [Nature 594, 365](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03546-8), preprint [arXiv 2201.10551](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10551)) fitted with RADMC-3D to the SPHERE images taken during the Great Dimming, drawn from the numbers they published. Their Extended Data Table 3 gives the December 2019 solution: a sphere of radius 6.5 au centred at (−1.9, −3.0, +12.5) au, of constant dust density 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ g cm⁻³, in MgFeSiO₄ grains centred on 0.21 µm. Their Extended Data Figure 6 defines the axes: x along right ascension, y along declination, z positive toward Earth. That places the clump south and slightly west of the star and between it and us, which is why the southern hemisphere went ten times darker.

The paper gives these in au at the distance it adopts, 222 pc, while this scene places the star at 168 pc. This package therefore reads them as angles at 222 pc and converts the angles to its own stellar radii: the sphere is 1.38 stellar radii in radius, centred 2.77 radii from the star's centre and 2.66 of them toward Earth. Dividing by this scene's own au per radius instead would draw the clump 1.32 times too far out and too large, which a first version did.

December 2019 is the only epoch shipped. The paper records its January and March 2020 solutions as unoptimised best guesses.

The clump is drawn as starlight scattered by that dust: the published density is uniform, so the brightness that varies across it is only the inverse-square illumination each parcel receives from the star. It is warm because scattered starlight is the star's own colour; nothing about its colour was measured.

## Preparation

`tools/objects/source-authoring/betelgeuse-shell/author.mts` samples each dataset into a 96³ density grid in `source/`: `density-zimpol-v.ktx2`, `density-emission-2020.ktx2`, `density-sio-2023.ktx2` and `density-veil-2019-12.ktx2`. It also writes the four volume recipes, the delivery, the presentation, the two dataset previews it draws itself and the provenance record, and it reads the SiO cube and continuum image that `reduce-alma-sio.mts` cuts from the archive's products. The nebula delivery's `density-grid` method bakes 24 slabs per axis, half a radius apart, through the Milky Way's slab baker, one lens per grid, and the ordinary nebula preparation installs the lens bank. The grids must share their bounds; the method refuses them otherwise.

## Where it appears

A cloud that surrounds the body it accompanies is not a place of its own, so this package ships no catalogue entry: it has no marker, no search result and no destination on the map. Its delivery names `attachedTo: "betelgeuse"` instead, and the bank it mounts stays dark until one of Betelgeuse's own datasets asks for it.

Betelgeuse's Datasets list therefore has four entries, and each draws the same sphere with at most one lens of this package around it. `matisse` is the reconstruction and draws `emission-2020`, its own light outside the disc. `dust-2024` draws `zimpol-v`, `dust-2019` draws `veil-2019-12` and `sio-2023` draws `sio-2023`; those three borrow the MATISSE dataset's prepared surface for the star, so they add no textures of their own. Selecting one asks the bank for its lens and turns the others off.

## How the lenses sit against the star

The volume normally composites behind the body, which would put a clump that occults the star behind it. A lens may therefore declare the centre of a compact structure that stands clear of the body in depth. The bank then moves that lens's cloud above or below the detail scene each frame, by which of the two is nearer to the camera. The 2019 clump declares one, and preparation refuses to write it unless every voxel of the grid is nearer to the observer than the photosphere. The 2024 map declares none, because its emission surrounds the star instead of standing clear of it, so it always draws behind. Nor do the 4 micrometre light or the SiO, which surround the star too, so the part of either that lies in front of the star still draws behind it.

That ordering is what lets the clump dim the star. The slab compiler takes absorption channels beside emission, so the clump's leaves carry their extinction as alpha and the light they scatter as colour, and ordinary source-over compositing gives transmission times the star behind plus the scattered term. The absorption is scaled from the paper's own report that the southern hemisphere went ten times darker: the line of sight through the clump's centre carries an optical depth of ln 10. The scattered light is drawn at four percent of the photosphere's surface brightness, which is a display choice and is marked as one.

What this still cannot do is cut a leaf at the limb. The clump covers the whole silhouette it crosses rather than being clipped by the sphere, which is exact here only because the clump stands clear of the photosphere in depth. Its extinction is grey; silicate dust reddens what it transmits, and no colour was applied because the photosphere behind it is drawn in an infrared intensity palette rather than in colour.

## Evidence

- The ESO, MATISSE and ALMA inputs are pinned by SHA-256 and size in `source/manifest.json`; the authoring script refuses a byte that differs, and `reduce-alma-sio.mts` rebuilds both ALMA inputs from the archive's products and fails unless they match their pins.
- The SiO registration is checked twice and the author fails if either check does: the star is the centroid of its own continuum, and the SiO absorption is centred 2.2 mas from it.
- Every number quoted here is measured by the script and recorded in `source/provenance.json#/measured`: the star and polarisation centres, floor and pixel scale; the fitted envelopes and their residuals; the star's position in the ALMA images, the SiO noise, mask, ring, velocities, absorption and depth placement; and the 2019 clump's converted centre and radius.
- East and west are checked on the rendered page. Betelgeuse's default view is pinned by its default-view test, with celestial north 42 degrees counterclockwise of screen-right and east 90 degrees beyond it. Rendered headless at that view on 18 September 2026 against the local dev server, the light the SiO dataset adds lies at 147 degrees, 1.4 disc radii out, where the ring's east-south-east weight predicts 164 and a mirrored volume would put it at 280. Every lens shares that frame. The frame and the measurement are in [evidence/east-west.webp](evidence/east-west.webp) and [evidence/east-west.json](evidence/east-west.json), rendered at revision `66cfa8331c`.
- At the default zoom the disc is 201 pixels in radius in a view 1000 pixels tall, so the view reaches 2.5 stellar radii above and below the star and most of the SiO ring, brightest at 2.4 and reaching four, lies outside it.

## Known problems

**Proof of concept.** This package exists to prove that a body can sit inside a prepared volume. It passes the application's availability gate on its own records; the local bypass an earlier draft needed is gone.

**No third axis for three of the four.** The 2024 shell and the 4 micrometre envelope are inferences from one projected profile each, not measurements, and the 2019 clump is a published model. Orbiting away from the observed direction shows the fitted shapes. The 2024 map's masked disc is spread along its shell the same way, so the shell has an empty cap toward Earth and another away from it: that is the mask made visible, not a structure. Only the SiO has a third axis from measurement: its velocities, placed on an assumed outflow.

**The intensity file is scaled.** Its header carries `BSCALE = 10.977`; the shared FITS reader applies it, raw byte reads do not, and only ratios to the peak are used here.

**Colour is a legend, not colour.** The degree of polarisation is a ratio and has no colour of its own. The leaves carry the colour map the paper prints it in, matplotlib `inferno`, on the paper's own zero-to-0.10 scale, so this dataset is their Fig. B.1 given depth rather than a picture of coloured dust. Four stops of the bar, its quarters, are carried as four emission channels with the stop colours; the slab compiler sums them, so a line of sight emits the interpolated bar colour of its own measured degree, and every channel shares the one depth profile, which keeps a column's chromaticity constant instead of only approximately so. Four stops are the most an RGBA8 grid can hold, so the bar is interpolated between quarters rather than sampled continuously. Brightness follows the renderer's `1 - exp(-gain x column)` transfer rather than the flat bar of a printed figure, so mid-scale values sit brighter than a linear colourbar would put them; the hue at every value is the published one. The instrumental floor is subtracted here and is not in the figure, so empty sky is darker than the print.

**The SiO ring is resolved, just.** The beam, 1.4 by 1.0 stellar radii, puts about 22 beams on the ring. One channel, 1.35 km/s, is about 0.15 stellar radii of depth where the ring crosses the sky plane and more toward the line of sight, and each sample is drawn narrower than that at its measured velocity. The continuum is removed by the pipeline and its residual by a per-pixel median, not a fitted baseline, and the cube is the archive's pipeline image, not re-imaged from visibilities.

**The 4 micrometre light starts 1.19 stellar radii out.** Within one beam of the disc it cannot be told from the star's own smeared edge. It is one reconstruction of one epoch.

## What is not verified

The depths. The 2024 shell and the 4 micrometre envelope are shapes fitted to one projection each, and the SiO velocities are placed on an outflow the absorption shows is not the whole story. Nothing here measures how far along the line of sight any of this material lies.

The SiO emission eight to fourteen stellar radii north of the star, and the faint continuum peak beside it, may be circumstellar or an artefact of the bright star in images made without self-calibration; the ledger keeps it open.
